AI Foundations

The vocabulary you need before anything else makes sense

01 – 12
01 / 100
🤖

Artificial Intelligence

Software that performs tasks normally requiring human intelligence — from recognising faces to writing essays, from driving cars to diagnosing disease.

Why it mattersIt's the umbrella term for the entire field. Every other concept on this list lives inside it.

Siri, ChatGPT, Tesla Autopilot and the spam filter in your inbox are all "AI" — but they use wildly different techniques.

02 / 100
📚

Machine Learning

A subfield where computers learn rules from data instead of being explicitly programmed with them. You show it examples, it figures out the pattern.

Why it mattersAlmost every modern AI success story — from recommendations to translation — is machine learning under the hood.

Feed a model 100,000 photos labelled "cat" or "dog" and it learns to tell them apart, without you writing a single rule.

03 / 100
⚡

Deep Learning

Machine learning using multi-layer neural networks. "Deep" = many layers, which lets the model learn rich, hierarchical patterns from raw data.

Why it mattersIt is the engine behind GPT, image recognition, voice assistants and self-driving cars. The 2010s AI revolution was a deep-learning revolution.

A 50-layer network can learn to recognise faces by first learning edges, then eyes, then whole faces — building understanding from simple parts.

04 / 100
🔗

Neural Network

Loosely brain-inspired math structures of connected "neurons" that transform inputs into outputs. Each connection has a learnable weight.

Why it mattersThe foundational architecture. Every modern AI is some flavour of neural network — the "lego brick" everything else is built from.

Input: pixel values. Hidden layers: math. Output: "this is a cat, 97% confident".

05 / 100
🧮

Algorithm

A precise recipe of steps a computer follows to solve a problem. AI is, at heart, a stack of algorithms — some hand-written, some learned from data.

Why it matters"AI is just algorithms" — understanding this demystifies the field. There's no magic, just math and data.

Sorting a list, finding the shortest route, recommending a video — all algorithms, all running inside AI systems.

06 / 100
📊

Data

The fuel of modern AI. Every model is shaped by the examples it sees during training — text, images, clicks, sensors, medical records.

Why it mattersMore and better data almost always beats a cleverer algorithm. The biggest AI labs compete as much on data as on talent.

GPT was trained on roughly a trillion words of public text. Without that data, no architecture would have produced ChatGPT.

07 / 100
🏷️

Training Data

The labelled or curated dataset used to teach a model. The single biggest determinant of how a model behaves, what it knows, and where it fails.

Why it matters"Garbage in, garbage out" is the oldest rule in ML. A biased dataset produces a biased model — every time.

A face-recognition system trained mostly on light-skinned faces will be measurably worse at darker-skinned faces. Documented, repeatable, fixable.

08 / 100
💾

Model

The output of training: a saved bundle of weights (numbers) that turns inputs into predictions. The "thing" you actually deploy.

Why it mattersWhen people say "GPT-4" or "Llama 3", they mean a specific model — a file you can run.

A trained spam-detection model is a ~50MB file of numbers that, given an email, outputs "spam" or "not spam" in milliseconds.

09 / 100
⚙️

Inference

Using a trained model to make a prediction on new, unseen data. This is the "running" phase — the part users actually experience.

Why it mattersTraining is expensive and slow. Inference is fast and cheap, and happens billions of times a day across the internet.

Every time you type a message in ChatGPT and hit enter, that's one inference. Each response might be hundreds of inferences in sequence.

10 / 100
🧪

Turing Test

Alan Turing's 1950 idea: if a human can't tell a machine from another human in text conversation, the machine "thinks". The original benchmark for AI.

Why it mattersIt shaped the entire field's goal for 70 years. Modern LLMs arguably pass it briefly — and that re-opened the entire question.

In 2014, a chatbot called "Eugene Goostman" fooled 33% of judges into thinking it was a 13-year-old Ukrainian boy. Controversial but technically a "pass".

11 / 100
❄️

AI Winter

Periods when funding and interest in AI collapsed because promises outpaced results. Two major ones: the late 80s and the mid-90s.

Why it mattersEvery "AI bubble" today is compared to past winters. History rhymes — and warns.

After expert systems failed to deliver in the 80s, AI research funding dropped by over 80% within five years. The field nearly died.

12 / 100
📜

Expert System

Early AI that encoded human expert rules as if/then statements. "If patient has fever AND cough, then consider infection." Powerful in narrow domains, brittle everywhere else.

Why it mattersThe dominant AI paradigm before machine learning took over. Shows what rule-based AI can and can't do.

MYCIN diagnosed bacterial infections in the 1970s and outperformed junior doctors — but couldn't explain its reasoning in a way humans trusted.

📊

Machine Learning

The toolkit that turned "AI is magic" into "AI is engineering"

13 – 25
13 / 100
👨‍🏫

Supervised Learning

Learning from examples where the correct answer is given. You hand the model (input, output) pairs and it learns the mapping.

Why it mattersThe most widely used form of ML in industry. Most "AI products" you use daily are supervised learning under the hood.

Predict house prices, classify emails as spam, detect fraudulent credit-card transactions. The pattern: lots of labelled past data → a model that generalises.

14 / 100
🔍

Unsupervised Learning

Finding structure in data with no labels. The model has to figure out what makes a "group" or "outlier" on its own.

Why it mattersMost real-world data is unlabelled. Unsupervised learning is how we make sense of it.

Customer segmentation: group shoppers by behaviour without telling the model what "VIP" or "discount-seeker" means — it discovers the segments itself.

15 / 100
🎮

Reinforcement Learning

Learning by trial, error and reward. An agent takes actions in an environment, gets rewards, and learns the policy that maximises total reward.

Why it mattersThe only paradigm that truly learns from interaction, not from a fixed dataset. Powers game-playing champions and robotics.

AlphaGo learned Go by playing itself millions of times, getting +1 for winning and -1 for losing, eventually beating the world champion 4-1.

16 / 100
⚖️

Semi-Supervised Learning

A small labelled set plus a big unlabelled set. The model learns from both, getting most of the benefit of supervision at a fraction of the labelling cost.

Why it mattersLabelling data is expensive. Semi-supervised learning is the pragmatic middle ground.

Label 1,000 medical scans out of 100,000 — get nearly the accuracy of having all 100,000 labelled.

17 / 100
🔄

Self-Supervised Learning

The model creates its own labels from raw data. Mask a word, predict it. Hide a frame, predict it. No humans needed.

Why it mattersThe trick that unlocked the modern LLM revolution. GPT and friends are essentially giant self-supervised learners.

BERT learned language by reading billions of sentences with random words blanked out, learning to fill in the gaps. No human labelled anything.

18 / 100
🏷️

Classification

Predicting a category: spam / not-spam, cat / dog / bird, positive / negative review, malignant / benign tumour.

Why it mattersHalf of all ML in production is classification. It's the workhorse of business AI.

Your bank's fraud system classifies every transaction in milliseconds. Your email's spam filter classifies billions of messages per day.

19 / 100
📈

Regression

Predicting a number: house price, temperature, sales next quarter, blood pressure, customer lifetime value.

Why it mattersWhenever the answer is "how much" or "how many", you want regression. Forecasts drive most business decisions.

Zillow's Zestimate predicts US home values using regression on hundreds of features — size, location, recent sales, school ratings.

20 / 100
🧩

Clustering

Grouping similar items without pre-defined labels. K-Means, DBSCAN, hierarchical clustering — the algorithms differ, the goal is the same.

Why it mattersIt's the "show me what's in this data" tool. Used in biology, marketing, anomaly detection and recommendation.

Spotify clusters millions of songs by audio features. Songs in the same cluster feel similar — even if no one labelled them that way.

21 / 100
🎯

Overfitting

When a model memorises the training data instead of learning the underlying pattern. Great on practice data, useless on new data.

Why it mattersThe most common beginner mistake and the central disease of ML. Detecting it is half the job.

A student who memorises past exam answers word-for-word will ace practice tests and fail the real one. That's overfitting in human form.

22 / 100
😴

Underfitting

The opposite of overfitting: the model is too simple to capture what's actually going on. It fails on both training and test data.

Why it mattersYou can't fix underfitting with more data. You need a more powerful model or better features.

Trying to predict house prices using only square footage — ignoring location, condition, age. The model is doomed no matter how much data you feed it.

23 / 100
⚖️

Bias-Variance Tradeoff

The central tension in ML: too rigid (high bias) and you miss; too flexible (high variance) and you memorise. The art is finding the sweet spot.

Why it mattersIt's the single most important mental model in all of machine learning. Almost every design decision trades one for the other.

A 1-degree polynomial is too biased to fit a curve. A 100-degree polynomial will wiggle through every point — and miss every new one. Aim for the 3rd degree.

24 / 100
✂️

Cross-Validation

Splitting data multiple ways to honestly test a model. The gold-standard way to report performance — and to pick between competing models.

Why it mattersA single train/test split can lie to you. Cross-validation gives a much more reliable estimate of real-world performance.

K-fold cross-validation: split your data into 5 chunks, train on 4, test on 1, repeat 5 times. Average the 5 scores. That's your honest number.

25 / 100
🎛️

Hyperparameter Tuning

Choosing the model's settings — learning rate, tree depth, layer count, regularisation strength. The art part of the science.

Why it mattersThe same algorithm with different hyperparameters can go from state-of-the-art to useless. This is where ML engineers spend their days.

A random forest with 10 trees is weak. With 1,000 trees it's great. With 100,000 trees it's slow and not better. Finding the right number is tuning.

⚡

Deep Learning

Where AI learned to see, hear, and read

26 – 38
26 / 100
⚪

Perceptron

The simplest neural network: inputs, weights, a threshold, an output. The 1958 seed from which everything else grew.

Why it mattersIt's the atom of deep learning. Understand this and every larger network is just more of the same, connected.

A perceptron can learn AND, OR, NOT — but famously can't learn XOR. That limitation triggered the first AI winter.

27 / 100
📚

Multilayer Perceptron

Stack many perceptrons in layers. Add non-linear activations between layers and you can approximate almost any function.

Why it mattersThe first "deep" network. The "MLP" is still the workhorse of tabular data — credit scoring, churn prediction, fraud detection.

A 3-layer MLP (input, hidden, output) can learn the XOR function that brought down the original perceptron. Add layers, add capability.

28 / 100
↩️

Backpropagation

The algorithm that updates a network's weights by sending the error backwards, layer by layer, using calculus (the chain rule).

Why it mattersBackprop is the reason deep learning works at all. Without it, training a 100-layer network would be impossible.

It's like a teacher grading a 100-question exam, then telling each student not just their overall score but which specific questions they got wrong.

29 / 100
🔌

Activation Function

ReLU, Sigmoid, GELU, Tanh — the non-linear switch at the end of each neuron that lets networks learn complex patterns.

Why it mattersWithout non-linearities, stacking layers is mathematically equivalent to a single layer. Activations are what make "deep" actually deep.

ReLU is just "if positive, pass through; else, zero". That one line of code powers most of modern AI.

30 / 100
⛰️

Gradient Descent

How models learn: nudge weights downhill on the loss surface, step by tiny step. Repeat millions of times.

Why it mattersIt's the optimisation engine at the heart of all deep learning. Every modern model is trained with some flavour of it.

Imagine you're blindfolded on a hill and want to reach the bottom. Feel the slope under your feet, take a small step downhill, repeat. That's gradient descent.

31 / 100
🖼️

Convolutional Neural Network

The architecture that cracked image recognition by sliding learned filters across pixels, building up from edges to objects.

Why it mattersCNNs made computer vision work in production. They're why your phone can recognise faces and why self-driving cars see pedestrians.

AlexNet won the 2012 ImageNet competition, halving the error rate of all previous approaches. That moment launched the deep-learning revolution.

32 / 100
🔁

Recurrent Neural Network

A network with memory, designed for sequences. Text, audio, time-series. Each step feeds into the next.

Why it mattersRNNs were the dominant sequence model for a decade and powered the first wave of machine translation, speech recognition and captioning.

Translating "I love AI" to French happens one word at a time, with the network remembering what came before. That's an RNN at work.

33 / 100
🚪

Long Short-Term Memory

An RNN variant with input, forget and output gates. Can remember information over long gaps in a sequence.

Why it mattersLSTM solved the "vanishing gradient" problem that made vanilla RNNs forget things after a few steps. It was the state of the art until Transformers.

"The cat, which I adopted from the shelter three years ago and that my sister named after her favourite musician, ___ very loud." LSTM can fill "is" — vanilla RNN can't.

34 / 100
⚡

Gated Recurrent Unit

A lighter, faster cousin of LSTM with fewer gates (reset + update). Same idea, fewer knobs.

Why it mattersGRUs train faster than LSTMs and often perform comparably. The default choice when you want a small, fast recurrent model.

On a small dataset, a GRU often matches an LSTM with half the parameters and twice the training speed.

35 / 100
🗜️

Autoencoder

A network that compresses data into a small code, then reconstructs it. Used for denoising, anomaly detection and feature learning.

Why it mattersAutoencoders are the foundation of generative models and dimensionality reduction. They teach the model what's important about the data.

Train an autoencoder on credit-card transactions. Fraudulent ones have high reconstruction error → flag them.

36 / 100
🎲

Dropout

Randomly switch off neurons during training so the network can't over-rely on any one path. A regularisation technique.

Why it mattersDropout is one line of code that dramatically reduces overfitting. It's in nearly every deep-learning model.

During training, 30% of neurons are randomly "off" each step. The network learns redundant pathways — and generalises far better.

37 / 100
📏

Batch Normalization

Keep layer activations in a healthy range so very deep networks can actually train. Originally a fix for an internal covariate shift problem.

Why it mattersBatchNorm made it practical to train 50-, 100-, even 1000-layer networks. Without it, training would be painfully slow and unstable.

Like resetting the thermostat in every room of a building so the heating system in the next room doesn't have to fight a -40°C input.

38 / 100
♻️

Transfer Learning

Take a model trained on one big task and fine-tune it for yours. 90% of practical AI starts here.

Why it mattersYou almost never train from scratch. You borrow a pre-trained model and adapt it — saving weeks of compute and millions of dollars.

Take a model trained on 1.4 million ImageNet photos, add 1,000 photos of your specific products, fine-tune. Works in an afternoon on a laptop.

💬

Natural Language Processing

How machines learned to read, write and reason with text

39 – 51
39 / 100
✂️

Tokenization

Splitting text into the chunks (tokens) a model can process — words, sub-words, or bytes. The first step in any NLP pipeline.

Why it mattersModels don't see "words" — they see tokens. How you tokenise affects model size, cost and even what languages work well.

GPT-4 tokenises "understanding" as ["under", "standing"]. "Unhappiness" becomes ["un", "happiness"]. Sub-word tokens handle any word, even made-up ones.

40 / 100
🧭

Word Embedding

Turning words into vectors of numbers so similar words land close together in a high-dimensional space.

Why it mattersEmbeddings let math operate on meaning. Once words are vectors, "king - man + woman = queen" is just vector arithmetic.

The words "cat", "kitten", "puppy" cluster together. "Paris", "Tokyo", "Mumbai" form their own cluster. Geography and zoology become geometry.

41 / 100
👑

Word2Vec

The 2013 method that proved words can be learned as vectors from raw text alone. King − Man + Woman ≈ Queen.

Why it mattersWord2Vec launched the modern NLP era. It showed that massive meaning can be extracted from raw text with almost no human labelling.

Trained on Google News, Word2Vec learned that "Paris" is to "France" as "Tokyo" is to "Japan". Pure math, no dictionary.

42 / 100
🎒

Bag of Words

The simplest text representation: count which words appear, ignore the order. Surprisingly useful as a baseline.

Why it mattersBoW is dumb and old — and still beats fancy models on small datasets. Always start here.

"Dog bites man" and "Man bites dog" have the same bag of words. Different meaning. Bag of Words can't tell — but for spam filtering, it often doesn't need to.

43 / 100
⚖️

TF-IDF

Weights a word by how unique it is to a document. Common words ("the", "is") get low weight; rare, meaningful words get high weight.

Why it mattersTF-IDF was the workhorse of search engines and spam filters for a decade. Cheap, fast, surprisingly competitive.

Searching "machine learning tutorial" — TF-IDF ranks documents where "machine" and "learning" appear together, down-weighting pages with the word "the" 500 times.

44 / 100
🔄

Transformer

The 2017 architecture that powers every modern LLM. "Attention is all you need" — literally the paper title.

Why it mattersThe single most important architecture of the decade. GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama, Mistral — all Transformers.

A Transformer reading a sentence looks at every word at once, deciding which other words matter for understanding each one. Parallel, not sequential — which is why it scales.

45 / 100
👁️

Self-Attention

Every token looks at every other token in the sequence and decides what to pay attention to. The core trick of the Transformer.

Why it mattersSelf-attention is what lets a model understand context — why "bank" means river bank in one sentence and money bank in another.

In "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired", self-attention figures out that "it" refers to "the animal", not "the street".

46 / 100
🧠

Multi-Head Attention

Several attention operations in parallel, each looking for different relationships. One head for syntax, one for meaning, one for long-range links, etc.

Why it mattersMultiple heads let the model capture many kinds of relationships in the same sentence — not just one.

In "I deposited money at the bank", one head might link "deposited" to "money", another links "bank" to "deposited" — different relationships, same layer.

47 / 100
📍

Positional Encoding

How Transformers know word order, since attention itself is position-blind. A clever sin/cos pattern added to each token's embedding.

Why it mattersWithout this, "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" are identical to a Transformer. Positional encoding fixes that.

Each token gets a unique position number encoded as a wave. The model can tell token #1 from token #2, and even how far apart they are.

48 / 100
🔎

BERT

Google's 2018 bidirectional encoder. Reads text both ways. Revolutionised search ranking and any "understand this text" task.

Why it mattersBERT changed Google Search in 2019 — affecting roughly 10% of all English queries. One model, billions of dollars of impact.

For "can you get medicine for someone pharmacy", BERT understands "for someone" is a context clue — better than keyword matching ever could.

49 / 100
✨

GPT

Generative Pre-trained Transformer. Predicts the next token, scaled to hundreds of billions of parameters. The "G" in ChatGPT.

Why it mattersGPT-3 (2020) and GPT-4 (2023) showed the world what scaling could do. The product that took AI mainstream.

GPT writes essays, code, emails, contracts, recipes. All from one simple training objective: predict the next word in a sentence.

50 / 100
📚

Large Language Model

An LLM: a Transformer trained on huge text corpora to predict the next token. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama, Mistral — all LLMs.

Why it mattersThe dominant AI of our era. A single architecture, trained at scale, can do translation, summarisation, reasoning, coding and conversation.

An LLM with 70 billion parameters can run on a laptop. A 1-trillion-parameter model can pass the bar exam. Size unlocks capability — but at a price.

51 / 100
🪄

Prompt Engineering

Crafting the input to an LLM to reliably get the output you want. The new programming.

Why it mattersSame model, better prompt = dramatically better results. Prompt engineering is now a real, high-paid skill.

"Write about dogs" gets generic output. "Write a 200-word essay in the style of a David Attenborough narration about a puppy meeting a tortoise" gets a specific, vivid result.

👁️

Computer Vision

Teaching machines to see the world the way we do

52 – 62
52 / 100
🏷️

Image Classification

Given an image, output one label. "This is a cat." The starting point of modern vision AI.

Why it mattersThe "Hello World" of computer vision. Every vision system builds on classification in some way.

Google Photos classifies every photo in your library — people, places, objects, even activities — and uses it for search.

53 / 100
📦

Object Detection

Find and box every object of interest. Not just "is there a dog" but "here is the dog, there is the cat, both at these pixels."

Why it mattersReal-world AI usually needs to know WHERE things are, not just WHAT. Object detection is the workhorse.

Self-driving cars detect every pedestrian, cyclist, vehicle, traffic sign and lane marking — usually 30 times per second.

54 / 100
🗺️

Image Segmentation

Mark every pixel that belongs to a thing, not just boxes. Semantic segmentation labels each pixel; instance segmentation separates each object.

Why it mattersFor medical imaging, AR effects and autonomous driving, boxes aren't precise enough. You need per-pixel precision.

In an MRI, segmentation can outline a tumour down to the pixel — giving surgeons exact 3D measurements to plan with.

55 / 100
👀

YOLO

"You Only Look Once" — the most famous real-time object detector. Fast enough to run on a phone, accurate enough to use in production.

Why it mattersYOLO made real-time object detection a commodity. It's the default for any project that needs vision at speed.

A YOLO model on a Raspberry Pi can detect people, cars and animals in a live video feed at 30 frames per second.

56 / 100
🖼️

R-CNN

The region-based CNN family: propose regions that might contain objects, then classify each. Slower than YOLO, often more accurate.

Why it mattersR-CNN started the modern object-detection race in 2014. Its descendants (Mask R-CNN, Faster R-CNN) are still used in research.

For medical scans where you need the most accurate possible bounding box, R-CNN variants are usually preferred over YOLO.

57 / 100
👤

Facial Recognition

Identify or verify a person from their face. A sensitive application — and a privacy flashpoint.

Why it mattersIt's the canonical example of AI's double edge: genuinely useful for security, genuinely dangerous for surveillance.

Your phone uses it to unlock. Airports use it for boarding. Authoritarian governments use it for surveillance. Same tech, very different worlds.

58 / 100
📃

Optical Character Recognition

Turning images of text into editable, searchable text. Receipts, books, license plates, handwritten notes.

Why it mattersOCR is the bridge between paper and digital. It's been around for decades but modern AI-OCR handles bad scans, handwriting and 100+ languages.

Drop a PDF of a 1990s scanned book into an OCR pipeline and get a fully editable, searchable digital version in minutes.

59 / 100
📐

Edge Detection

Finding where pixels change sharply — the first step in almost every vision pipeline. The Canny edge detector is the classic.

Why it mattersEdges = structure. Find the edges, and the rest of vision becomes much easier. Even modern CNNs use edge-like filters in their first layer.

Self-driving systems use edge detection to find lane markings — even in rain, at night, with worn paint.

60 / 100
🔬

Feature Extraction

Turning raw pixels into a useful, compact representation. The step where most of the "intelligence" lives.

Why it mattersA good feature extractor turns an impossible problem into a trivial one. Deep learning's biggest contribution is "let the model learn its own features".

For face recognition, hand-crafted features used to be distances between eyes, nose width and so on. CNNs learn their own — better than any human can design.

61 / 100
🧩

Convolutional Layer

The learnable filter that slides across an image, detecting edges, shapes, then objects. The building block of every CNN.

Why it mattersConvolution is what makes vision networks work. It exploits the fact that nearby pixels are related, and the same pattern can appear anywhere.

A 3x3 filter that detects vertical edges will fire whether the edge is in the top-left or bottom-right of the image. Translation invariance, built in.

62 / 100
📉

Pooling Layer

Downsamples feature maps to keep computation manageable and the model robust to small position shifts. Max pooling is the standard.

Why it mattersWithout pooling, every layer would produce massive outputs. Pooling shrinks them — and gives the model "I don't care exactly where" flexibility.

A 2x2 max-pool takes a 4x4 feature map and outputs the strongest value from each 2x2 block. The model learns "is there an eye here?" not "is the eye at exactly pixel 47, 52?"

🎨

Generative AI

From analysing the world to creating new ones

63 – 75
63 / 100
⚔️

Generative Adversarial Network

Two networks duel: a Generator fakes images, a Discriminator judges them. The result is photorealistic synthesis.

Why it mattersGANs were the first model family to generate images indistinguishable from real photos. They launched the generative-AI era in 2014.

ThisPersonDoesNotExist.com shows a fresh AI-generated face every refresh — all from a StyleGAN model. None of them are real people.

64 / 100
📦

Variational Autoencoder

An autoencoder that learns a smooth, sampleable latent space. The grandparent of modern image generators.

Why it mattersVAEs introduced the idea of a continuous, meaningful "space" you can sample from — the foundation of all modern generative models.

The "smile slider" in face-editing apps comes from a VAE. Move along one direction in latent space and smiles appear — without anyone labelling "smile".

65 / 100
🌫️

Diffusion Model

Learn to reverse a noising process. Add noise step by step, then teach a model to undo it. The technique behind Stable Diffusion, DALL-E 3, and Imagen.

Why it mattersDiffusion models are the current state of the art for image and video generation. They produce sharper, more controllable results than GANs.

Midjourney v6, DALL-E 3, Stable Diffusion XL and Adobe Firefly are all diffusion models under the hood. Different products, same core idea.

66 / 100
🖼️

DALL-E

OpenAI's text-to-image model. Sparked the consumer generative-AI wave in 2022.

Why it mattersWhen DALL-E 2 launched, it broke the public's mental model of what AI could do. Suddenly, anyone could "draw" with words.

"An astronaut riding a horse in a cyberpunk city, oil painting" — DALL-E generates four images in ~10 seconds.

67 / 100
🌍

Stable Diffusion

Open-source diffusion model. The community's workhorse — fine-tunes, LoRAs, ControlNets, all built on this.

Why it mattersBecause it's open, Stable Diffusion spawned an entire ecosystem of fine-tunes for styles, characters and use cases no lab would ever train for.

Train a LoRA on 20 photos of your dog → Stable Diffusion can now generate infinite images of your dog in any style, anywhere.

68 / 100
🎭

Midjourney

The Discord-native image generator famous for its painterly, cinematic default look. Closed-source, opinionated aesthetics.

Why it mattersMidjourney defined what "AI art" looks like to most people. Its default style is gorgeous out of the box.

Most viral AI images you've seen on social media in 2023-24 are Midjourney. Its V6 model is the gold standard for stylised illustration.

69 / 100
✍️

Text-to-Image

The whole category: write a prompt, get a picture. The most visible face of generative AI.

Why it mattersText-to-image collapsed the skill floor for visual creation. You no longer need to draw to "make" an image — you need to describe.

A book author can now generate a unique cover for their novel in 30 seconds, for the cost of a few cents of compute.

70 / 100
🎬

Text-to-Video

Describe a scene, get a video. Sora, Veo, Runway Gen-3, Kling, Pika — all racing here in 2026.

Why it mattersVideo is the next frontier. Models can now generate short, coherent, high-quality clips from a sentence.

"A drone shot over a misty forest at sunrise, cinematic" → a 10-second 1080p video in under a minute. Hollywood is paying close attention.

71 / 100
🎨

Neural Style Transfer

Re-render one image in the artistic style of another. The "Prisma filter" trick, mathematically.

Why it mattersIt was the first viral demonstration that neural networks could "understand" art style, not just recognise objects.

Take your holiday photo and a Van Gogh painting, get a version of your photo that looks like Van Gogh painted it. The 2016 viral sensation.

72 / 100
🔍

Super-Resolution

AI upscaling: turn a small, blurry image into a large, sharp one. Now standard in phones and TVs.

Why it mattersBigger images without bigger files. Or restore old family photos from low-res scans. Or stream 4K video with 1080p bandwidth.

The latest Samsung phones "zoom" beyond their optical zoom by using a super-resolution model. The blur becomes detail.

73 / 100
🎭

Deepfake

Synthetic media so realistic it fools the eye. A technology, a threat, and a legal headache all at once.

Why it mattersDeepfakes are the AI safety question everyone can see. They're used in entertainment, fraud, election interference and identity theft.

A 2024 deepfake video call cost a Hong Kong firm $25 million. The CFO was real-looking but AI-generated. Real money, real lesson.

74 / 100
🎙️

Voice Cloning

Replicate a voice from a few seconds of audio. ElevenLabs, Tortoise, OpenVoice and open models all do it now.

Why it mattersSame double edge as deepfakes: brilliant for accessibility (people who lost their voice) and terrifying for fraud.

A patient with ALS used ElevenLabs to clone their voice from old recordings — then spoke through it for the rest of their life. Same tech, used for love.

75 / 100
🎵

Music Generation

AI that composes. Suno, Udio, and open models like MusicGen. A genuine new instrument — and a copyright question.

Why it mattersMusic generation democratised songwriting. It's also the front line of the largest copyright lawsuit in AI history.

"Lo-fi hip-hop beats to study to, 3 minutes, female vocals" → a complete, original track in 30 seconds. SoundCloud is now full of them.

🤖

Agents & Applications

From answering questions to actually getting things done

76 – 87
76 / 100
🤖

AI Agent

An LLM that doesn't just chat — it plans, uses tools, and acts over many steps to hit a goal.

Why it mattersAgents are the bridge from "AI as a typewriter" to "AI as a coworker". They turn language models into doers.

"Book me a flight to Mumbai under ₹15,000 next Tuesday" — an agent searches, compares, picks, fills forms, pays, sends confirmation. All autonomously.

77 / 100
🌀

Autonomous Agent

An agent with minimal human input, running long workflows end to end. Auto-GPT started the wave in 2023.

Why it mattersAutonomy is the difference between "AI assistant" and "AI employee". The business implications are enormous.

Give an autonomous agent a goal like "research my competitors and write a report" — it plans sub-tasks, browses, takes notes, writes, iterates.

78 / 100
📚

Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Let the LLM look things up in your documents before answering. The fix for "the model doesn't know my data".

Why it mattersRAG is how every enterprise uses LLMs. It grounds the model's answers in real, current, private data.

Customer support bot for a bank: a RAG system finds the relevant policy doc, hands it to the LLM, the LLM writes a grounded answer with citations.

79 / 100
🗄️

Vector Database

A database tuned for similarity search over embeddings. Pinecone, Weaviate, pgvector, Chroma, Qdrant.

Why it mattersThe "memory" of every RAG system. Store embeddings, search by meaning instead of keywords.

A vector database holding 10 million product descriptions can find "cozy autumn sweaters" even if no product has those exact words.

80 / 100
🧭

Embedding Space

The high-dimensional space where meaning lives. Near = similar. Far = unrelated. The geometry of understanding.

Why it mattersAlmost every modern AI feature — search, recommendations, RAG, clustering — depends on a good embedding space.

"I love this movie" and "This film is amazing" are far apart in word space but close in embedding space. The second is what matters.

81 / 100
💬

Chatbot

Software that converses. From 1960s ELIZA to today's multimodal assistants. Same name, very different beast.

Why it mattersThe most common way people interact with AI. Every business is being asked "do you have a chatbot for that?".

Banking apps, airline websites, and your doctor's appointment system all use chatbots. The good ones are LLM-powered; the bad ones are still 1990s decision trees.

82 / 100
🎯

Recommendation System

"Because you watched X..." Collaborative filtering, content-based, hybrid — the engine of Netflix, YouTube, Amazon, Spotify.

Why it mattersRecommender systems drive an estimated 30-40% of consumer web revenue. They shape what you see, buy and watch.

TikTok's "For You" feed is famously the most aggressive recommender ever shipped. It's the reason the average user spends 95 minutes a day on the app.

83 / 100
🔮

Predictive Analytics

Using historical data to forecast the future. Churn, demand, risk, equipment failure, weather, sales.

Why it mattersMost business "AI" is actually predictive analytics. It's where data science meets operations.

A retailer predicts next month's demand per store per SKU. Cuts stockouts by 30%, cuts overstock by 25%. Multi-million dollar impact, classic ML model.

84 / 100
🎤

Speech Recognition

Audio in, text out. Whisper, Deepgram, and the dictation in your phone — all neural networks now.

Why it mattersVoice is the most natural interface. Modern speech recognition is good enough to replace a keyboard for many tasks.

OpenAI's Whisper transcribes 99 languages with near-human accuracy. Doctors using it save 2 hours of paperwork per day.

85 / 100
🔊

Text-to-Speech

Text in, lifelike voice out. ElevenLabs, PlayHT, Azure Neural voices, and the voices inside every GPS and audiobook app.

Why it mattersIndistinguishable from a human voice now. Audiobooks, dubbing, accessibility, call-centre automation — all changing fast.

An AI can read a 300-page novel aloud in any voice, in any language, with emotion. The audiobook industry is being rebuilt.

86 / 100
👁️

Computer Vision

The field of teaching machines to see. A whole discipline — and one of AI's biggest commercial wins.

Why it mattersFrom medical imaging to quality control on factory lines, vision AI is saving industries billions.

A tomato-sorting facility uses computer vision to grade every tomato for ripeness, size and defects. Replaces dozens of human sorters, with higher accuracy.

87 / 100
🦾

Robotics

Embodied AI: perception, planning, action. From warehouse arms to humanoids like Optimus and Figure 02.

Why it mattersAI that moves in the real world is the next industrial revolution. Labour, logistics, healthcare, home — all up for grabs.

Amazon's warehouses use over 750,000 robots. The latest humanoids can fold laundry, sort packages and even (slowly) make espresso.

Ethics, Safety & Future

What we build, what we should build, and where this is all going

88 – 100
88 / 100
🌀

AI Hallucination

When a model confidently invents facts. The single biggest problem in production LLM use today.

Why it mattersLawyers have filed cases citing fake cases. Doctors have been given fake medical references. Hallucinations make LLMs dangerous when used naively.

A New York lawyer used ChatGPT for legal research. It invented six fake cases. He was sanctioned. Real consequence, real lawyer, real fine.

89 / 100
⚖️

Algorithmic Bias

When a system systematically disadvantages certain groups because of skewed training data, design choices or feedback loops.

Why it mattersAI doesn't just reflect bias — it can amplify it, at scale, invisibly. From hiring tools to medical risk scores, biased AI causes real harm.

Amazon's AI hiring tool down-ranked resumes containing the word "women's" (e.g. "women's chess club"). It learned from 10 years of male-dominated hires.

90 / 100
🎯

AI Alignment

The research problem of making powerful AI do what we actually want — including what we mean but didn't say. The hardest problem in AI safety.

Why it mattersAs AI gets more capable, the cost of misalignment grows. A model that pursues the wrong goal at scale can cause catastrophic harm.

The "paperclip maximiser" thought experiment: an AI told to make paperclips might convert the entire planet into paperclip factories. The problem isn't intelligence, it's alignment.

91 / 100
🔦

Explainable AI

Methods that make a model's decisions interpretable to humans. SHAP, LIME, attention maps, surrogate models.

Why it mattersFor medical, legal and financial AI, "because the model said so" is not an acceptable answer. Regulators now require explanations.

A bank denying a loan application must, by law in the EU, explain why. SHAP values let the model say "income was 60% of the reason, debt-to-income 30%".

92 / 100
🛡️

AI Safety

The broader field studying catastrophic and long-term risks from advanced AI systems. Bio, cyber, autonomy, persuasion.

Why it mattersFrontier AI models can now help non-experts build bioweapons, hack systems and run influence operations. That's a new kind of risk.

A red-team test in 2023 found GPT-4 could walk a non-expert through synthesising a known dangerous compound. The lab changed the model.

93 / 100
🎯

Adversarial Attack

Tiny, crafted perturbations that fool a model. A sticker on a stop sign, a few pixels on a panda, a single character in a prompt.

Why it mattersAdversarial attacks expose how fragile models are. They matter for self-driving cars, malware detection, biometric security.

Place four small black-and-white stickers on a stop sign and a Tesla will read it as a 45 mph sign. Demonstrated in real conditions.

94 / 100
🔒

Data Privacy

What AI learns from your data, who can see it, and what you can do about it. GDPR, CCPA, the new rules.

Why it mattersEvery AI feature is, secretly, a data question. Your photos train face recognition. Your messages train autocomplete. Your choices train recommenders.

Italy temporarily banned ChatGPT in 2023 over privacy concerns. The company added age verification, a clear privacy policy, and an opt-out.

95 / 100
📜

AI Regulation

The EU AI Act, US executive orders, India's digital rules, China's generative AI measures. The law catching up to the technology.

Why it mattersEvery AI product now ships with a legal question attached. "Is this allowed?" depends on country, use case and risk level.

The EU AI Act (2024) bans social scoring and real-time biometric ID by default. The first comprehensive AI law in history.

96 / 100
🌟

Artificial General Intelligence

Hypothetical AI that matches or exceeds humans across every cognitive task. The field's north star.

Why it mattersEvery major lab is publicly working toward AGI. Whether it's 5 years or 50 away, the goal shapes billions of dollars of investment today.

Sam Altman, Demis Hassabis and Dario Amodei — the CEOs of OpenAI, DeepMind and Anthropic — all believe AGI is plausible within a decade. So does the US government.

97 / 100
🧠

Superintelligence

The further step beyond AGI: an intellect vastly smarter than the best human in every field. Science, strategy, social manipulation — all.

Why it mattersIf we build it, we need to have aligned it. That's a research problem no one has solved. The stakes are existential by definition.

Nick Bostrom's "Superintelligence" (2014) put this question on the mainstream map. It's now a serious academic and policy field.

98 / 100
🚀

Singularity

The theoretical point where technological growth becomes runaway and unpredictable — driven by AI improving AI.

Why it mattersWhether you think the singularity is near, far or never, the term shapes how seriously people take long-term AI risk.

Ray Kurzweil predicts 2045 as the singularity date. Most AI researchers think it's later or won't happen. The debate itself is the story.

99 / 100
⚛️

Quantum AI

Where quantum computing meets machine learning. Promises speed-ups for certain problems; mostly still research.

Why it mattersSome problems — drug discovery, materials science, optimisation — are naturally quantum. AI that uses quantum computers could tackle them.

Google's quantum chip solved a problem in 5 minutes that would take a classical supercomputer 10 septillion years. That kind of speed could transform AI training.

100 / 100
🧬

Neuromorphic Computing

Hardware that mimics how biological brains work — spiking, event-driven, ultra-low-power. The long-term bet.

Why it mattersToday's GPUs waste enormous energy running neural networks. Neuromorphic chips run them with brain-like efficiency.

Intel's Hala Point, the largest neuromorphic system, runs 1.15 billion neurons with 100x less energy than a GPU cluster. The future of edge AI.
